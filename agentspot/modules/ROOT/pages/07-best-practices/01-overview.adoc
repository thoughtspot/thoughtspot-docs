What good AgentSpot agents and workflows have in common — the patterns we keep seeing in production deployments, condensed.

== The shape of a good agent

Five attributes show up over and over:

* *Narrow scope.* It does one job for one audience.
* *Short instructions.* Under 300 words. Specific. Bulleted.
* *Minimal data surface.* Two or three relevant data sources, not twenty.
* *Few skills.* If skills exist, instructions explicitly say when to use each.
* *Explicit fallbacks.* "If I don't know, I say so and suggest who to ask."

The agents that fail are usually broad, long-prompted, over-attached to data, and full of skills the prompt doesn't tell them when to use.

== The shape of a good workflow

* *Small.* Three to seven steps. If it's longer, split it.
* *One verb per step.* Each step does one thing.
* *Named clearly.* `Summarize yesterday's deals`, not `Step 3`.
* *Observable.* Failures alert someone who'll see them today, not in a quarterly review.
* *Human in the loop where it matters.* Approval steps in front of irreversible actions during the trust-building phase.
* *Reversible where possible.* Idempotent operations beat one-shot writes when the downstream system supports it.

== A 30-day pattern that works

When a team is new to AgentSpot, this rollout consistently outperforms the more ambitious alternatives:

. *Week 1: One narrow agent.* Build a single, focused, internal agent. Publish private. Use it personally. Iterate.
. *Week 2: Share to a small group.* Two or three colleagues. Collect feedback via thumbs-down. Publish a v2 that addresses the top three complaints.
. *Week 3: First workflow.* Take a recurring, low-stakes task someone does manually each week and turn it into a workflow. Keep a *Wait for approval* on the final write step.
. *Week 4: Promote the agent to a wider group.* Remove the approval step from the workflow once you trust it. Document what was built.

Teams that try to build five agents and three workflows in the first two weeks reliably end up with a pile of half-tested artifacts and a bad first impression.

== The four anti-patterns

The most common failure modes, in order of how often we see them:

. *Do-everything agent.* Started narrow, accumulated scope across versions, now does eight things badly instead of one well.
. *Prompt as documentation.* Long instructions that read like a Confluence page; the model ignores most of it.
. *No fallback.* Agent has nothing to say when the answer isn't in the data, so it guesses confidently.
. *Workflow with no observability.* Failures pile up silently for weeks until someone notices the digest stopped arriving.

== A short maintenance routine

Once an agent or workflow is live, the maintenance cost is small but non-zero. Reserve thirty minutes a month per active artifact:

* Read the last two weeks of thumbs-down feedback. Group the complaints.
* Glance at the audit log for failures or unusual patterns.
* Check whether the underlying data has changed shape (new columns, retired tables, schema drift).
* Decide: keep, edit, or retire.

Artifacts that survive twelve monthly reviews are usually production-grade. Most don't.
